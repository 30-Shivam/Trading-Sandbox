Fixed: allocate_capital() caps any one sector at max_sector_allocation_pct (default 40%) of your WHOLE portfolio value (today's cash + Current Holdings, not just today's cash pool in isolation) via sector_lookup + existing_holdings. 2. [DONE] Backtest realism. Was: catalyst/earnings timing wasn't simulated (so the "69% win rate" number never saw the volatility real Catalyst_Warning trades have), and there was no slippage/commission modeling. Fixed: settle_trade() now applies config.slippage_pct (0.1% default, only on stop_hit_intraday fills -- gap fills already use the real Open, target fills are limits that can't legitimately execute worse) and config.commission_pct_per_trade (0.0 default, % of trade value). run_backtest.fetch_earnings_dates() pulls ~10 years of real REPORTED earnings dates (only the calendar date, never EPS/Surprise, which would be real leakage) so Catalyst_Warning is now honestly simulated (--with-catalyst flag) instead of always False. Genuinely humbling result along the way: a small 4-ticker/3-year smoke test showed catalyst trades performing catastrophically worse (35.71% win rate vs. 72.22% non-catalyst) -- but a larger 15-ticker/5-year sample (457 catalyst trades vs. 14) showed the OPPOSITE (79.43% vs. 73.24%), proving the small sample was just noise.

3. [DONE] Backtest/live trust gap -- full writeup in backtest_diagnostic.txt. Every backtest one night reported 66-88% win rates; 63 real settled live trades showed 31.75%. Root cause confirmed: the live sample was all from a 3-trading-day window (07-24 to 07-28), 82.5% Technology, during an active sector-wide decline -- not a representative sample, not a fundamental break. Concrete evidence this was right: the very next day's settlement run (after fixing the bug below) resolved 23 new trades, nearly all WINs from a sharp reversal, moving the live win rate from 31.75% to 50.0% overnight. Two things fixed: (a) settle_trades.py now reconstructs each trade's own config_snapshot instead of hardcoding DEFAULT_CONFIG, so a trade is graded against the exit policy actually in effect when it was entered, not retroactively reinterpreted. (b) Built check_survivorship_bias.py + sp500_membership.py (cached point-in-time S&P 500 membership data, MIT licensed, github.com/hanshof/sp500_constituents, 1996-2025-08-23) to actually MEASURE the suspected watchlist-curation bias instead of just asserting it -- ran 3 independent 30-ticker seeds (42, 7, 99) after the first seed alone turned out to be premature evidence (same small-sample-flip mistake as the catalyst-warning finding, made twice). Results: seed 42 favored the point-in-time sample (-1.11pp win rate for watchlist), seed 7 favored the watchlist (+4.02pp), seed 99 favored point-in-time again (-1.26pp) -- averaging to +0.55pp win rate / -0.027 sharpe_like, both close to zero. Conclusion: watchlist curation bias, if it exists, is small relative to the sampling noise at n=30 and is NOT the dominant driver of the original gap -- that remains the sector-concentration/narrow-window finding. One residual, unfixed caveat, consistent across all 3 seeds: 3/30 point-in-time tickers were completely unfetchable via yfinance each time (delisted/acquired/renamed since 2021) -- genuinely failed companies remain a structural blind spot no amount of re-sampling fixes. Also found and fixed a real bug along the way: compute_levels() crashed with a raw TypeError instead of a clean RuntimeError on genuinely bad ticker data (a reused/renamed symbol with sparse history).

4. [DONE] Fill-confirmation gap. Was: the system had no way to know whether a logged signal turned into a real fill -- every Strong Buy/Buy was walked forward as if entered exactly at Buy_Price. Fixed: new confirm_fill.py CLI (list pending unconfirmed signals; confirm one with --ticker/--date and an optional --price if your real fill differed from the logged buy_price). Trade_Signals gained confirmed_filled/fill_price/confirmed_at (deliberately NOT part of log_trade_signal's upsert $set, so a re-scan can never silently wipe a confirmation -- verified directly). settle_trades.py now uses fill_price as the real entry price when set (Stop_Loss/Sell_Price never change -- they're absolute levels from signal time) and carries confirmed_filled onto the Trade_Outcomes document; its summary and optimize.py's live-outcomes report now both split "every mechanical signal" from "confirmed real fills only." Real edge case found and fixed: confirming a fill AFTER a signal already settled left the old Trade_Outcomes doc stale (wrong entry_price, missing confirmed_filled) since it wouldn't naturally get re-walked -- confirm_fill.py now re-settles immediately in that case, verified against the real MRVL trade (entry_price/pnl_pct correctly updated in place).

5. [DONE] Correlation-adjusted backtest statistics. Was: summarize_trades()'s mean/std treated every pooled trade as an independent sample, but a real observed failure mode is dozens of correlated Technology signals firing the same day during one sector move -- that's one correlated event counted as if it were dozens of independent data points, understating true variance and overstating sharpe_like's apparent confidence. Fixed: new swingtrade.compute_cluster_weights() gives each (entry_date, sector) cluster a combined weight of 1.0 split evenly across members (conservative -- assumes perfect intra-cluster correlation, zero inter-cluster); new swingtrade.summarize_trades_weighted() is the shared weighted-stats core (Kish effective sample size), now used by both optimize.py's summarize_weighted (combined with the existing recency weight) and by run_backtest.py/evaluate_config.py's reporting (cluster weight alone, printed alongside the raw numbers for comparison). Verified on a synthetic 10-trade correlated cluster: naive pooling reported sharpe_like=1.953 (11/12 wins, wildly optimistic); cluster-adjusted correctly collapsed it to effective_trade_count=4.3, sharpe_like=0.56. Real run_backtest.py smoke test on 7 tickers showed a modest 10% inflation (small sample, only 2 sectors); the effect will be much larger on the full 100-ticker/11-sector watchlist where 30+ same-day Technology signals have been directly observed. Real end-to-end optimize.py run confirmed no regressions.

Hypothesis confirmed. BUT: re-scored v3's exact current params under the same corrected objective for a fair comparison, and v3 (effective_trade_count=854.4, sharpe_like=0.941) is statistically tied with the new best trial (effective_trade_count=60.2, sharpe_like=0.934) -- fixing the artifact didn't reveal a better config, it just stopped the search from being fooled by one. 7. [DONE] Entry-fill timing realism. Was: the backtest entered a simulated trade on the same bar the signal fired, using that same bar's own Buy_Price as if it were instantly and perfectly filled -- but a signal computed from day D's close can't be acted on until day D+1 at the earliest, and even then only if price actually comes back down to touch the resting limit order. Fixed: new swingtrade.backtest._find_entry_fill() walks forward from the day after the signal (gap-aware: a gap-down through the limit fills at the better/lower real Open; an intraday touch fills at exactly Buy_Price) within a new config.max_entry_wait_days (default 5 trading days) -- if the limit is never touched in that window, the signal now correctly produces NO trade instead of a phantom one. stop_loss/sell_price are re-anchored to the REAL fill price, not the stale signal-time Buy_Price.

   Real bug caught and fixed along the way: the first implementation still read Stop_Loss/Sell_Price from the ORIGINAL signal-time values even when the actual fill happened at a different (gap-down, lower) price -- meaning a gap-filled entry's stop could sit ABOVE its actual entry, triggering near-instant fake stop-outs. A 7-ticker smoke test surfaced this as an implausible win-rate collapse (66.99% -> 34.85%); fixing the stale-anchor bug only partially recovered it (-> 39.39%), proving a second, legitimate (non-bug) effect was also present: a direct trade-level diff showed the strict-limit model was systematically dropping the CLEANEST bounces (the 33 signals it excluded would have been 100% winners under naive same-bar entry, averaging +7.32% over 5 days) -- exactly the trades where price never came back down to the limit because it was already reversing. Surfaced this to you explicitly via AskUserQuestion (strict limit order vs. market-style next-open entry vs. a togglable dual-mode); you chose to keep the strict limit-order model as the permanent, single behavior -- it's the honest one, even though it's the harsher one.

   Full-scale result (same 15-ticker/56-fold sample used throughout this session's validation work, v3's exact currently-active params, entry-fill + correlation + recency all combined): win_rate collapsed from v3's previously-reported 69.18% to 26.74% weighted / 27.99% raw (1147 trades, effective_trade_count=251.2); sharpe_like collapsed from 0.826/0.941 to 0.122/0.098. avg_pnl_pct stayed marginally positive (+0.45%/+0.38% per trade) -- consistent with an asymmetric RRR strategy (tight stops, wider targets) still being net-positive on average despite a sub-30% win rate, just far less "reliable-feeling" than the old number implied. Sanity-checked: win_count(321)+loss_count(817)+expired_count(9)=1147=trade_count, arithmetic is internally consistent; this is a deterministic re-score of the same config on the same data (no seed/sampling randomness involved), so it isn't subject to the small-sample-noise caveat that applied to the survivorship/catalyst findings. Partial cross-check against real life: live settled trades sit at 50% win rate -- much closer to this corrected 27% estimate than to the old backtest's 69%, though not an exact match (live sample is still small/narrow-window per point 3 above). This is the single most consequential finding of the whole accuracy investigation: v3's believed edge was substantially an artifact of assuming perfect, instantaneous limit fills. v3 remains active (nothing better has been found yet under the corrected model), but its true expected performance should now be understood as much weaker than previously believed, and the next natural step is a fresh Optuna search under this fully-corrected objective to see whether different parameters (e.g. different support_lookback_days/max_entry_wait_days interactions) can do meaningfully better than v3 once execution is modeled honestly.

8. [DONE] Ticker-universe held-out validation for Optuna. Was: walk-forward already held out TIME rigorously (in-sample/out-of-sample folds, proven no-look-ahead), but every search also tuned AND "validated" on the exact same ticker universe -- a config could be quietly overfit to the specific names in the watchlist without anyone catching it. Fixed: new optimize.split_tickers_holdout() partitions fetched tickers into a TUNE set (all trials/Optuna search only ever see this) and a HOLDOUT set stratified by sector (so neither set concentrates in one sector -- the exact mechanism behind the original live-vs-backtest gap) that Optuna never touches during search. After the winner is picked, both it and DEFAULT_CONFIG get re-run once against the holdout tickers over the same folds, reported side by side and stored on the candidate doc as holdout_metrics (storage.write_candidate gained this param, additive, doesn't disturb promote_config.py's existing table view). --holdout-frac (default 0.25) / --holdout-seed (default 42) control it; --holdout-frac 0 disables (old behavior).

   Validated in two stages, same discipline as every other feature this session: a 15-ticker/3-trial smoke test proved the split logic (deterministic, seed-sensitive, no overlap, sector-stratified) and the reporting plumbing worked end-to-end, but also immediately proved WHY a small ticker pool doesn't work for this specific kind of validation -- 25% of 15 tickers rounded to a single holdout ticker (MU, ~12 trades), correctly triggering the code's own thin-sample warning. Real answer required the full 108-ticker watchlist (100 fetched, 8 unfetchable -- same delisted/renamed blind spot noted in point 3): 73 tune / 27 holdout tickers, 56 folds, 8-trial search (trimmed from a planned 15 after the first attempt was killed mid-run by a session boundary interrupting the background process -- see below). Result was clean and consequential: the winning trial (#3) beat DEFAULT_CONFIG baseline on the TUNE tickers (sharpe_like=0.205 vs. 0.165, win_rate 48.46% vs. 43.85%) -- but on the 27 HOLDOUT tickers it never saw during tuning, the SAME winning config actually did WORSE than baseline (sharpe_like=-0.128 vs. -0.041, win_rate 34.28% vs. 35.34%), with a comfortably large holdout sample (effective_trade_count=79.6, well above the 15-trade trust floor). That is textbook ticker-level overfitting, caught red-handed by the exact mechanism this feature exists to build -- confirms the concern was real, not theoretical. Also notable: BOTH configs are net-negative (sharpe_like < 0) on the holdout tickers under the entry-fill-realistic model, independently reinforcing point 7's finding that this system's true edge is much thinner than the old numbers implied, via a completely different methodology (ticker holdout, not time holdout). Written as candidate v9, explicitly NOT promoted -- it demonstrably fails to generalize to unseen tickers. Caveat: this run compared the candidate against DEFAULT_CONFIG's holdout score, not v3's (the currently-active config) -- v3 has never itself been validated against a held-out ticker set, so whether v3 ITSELF is ticker-overfit remains an open question, not yet answered.

   Operational lesson, not a code bug: a background optimize.py run does NOT survive a session/harness restart in this environment -- the first full-scale attempt (15 trials) was silently killed after 2 trials (~66 minutes of real compute) when the session was interrupted, with no way to resume from where it left off (trial results aren't checkpointed anywhere until the whole study finishes). Relaunched at a trimmed 8 trials specifically to reduce the odds of losing progress to another interruption. Long unattended optimize.py runs should stay mindful of this until/unless trial-level checkpointing is added.

   Open question resolved: does v3 (the active config) show the same ticker-overfitting v9 did? Re-scored v3's exact params against the SAME 27 holdout tickers/folds/methodology (re-verified DEFAULT_CONFIG's holdout score reproduced exactly: sharpe_like=-0.041, confirming no methodology drift). Result: v3 on holdout = sharpe_like=-0.024, win_rate=20.88%, trade_count=2021, effective_trade_count=431.9 (very large, trustworthy sample). Comparing all three on the identical 27 tickers -- DEFAULT_CONFIG -0.041, candidate v9 -0.128, v3 -0.024 -- v3 does NOT show v9's red flag; it's actually the LEAST negative of the three despite by far the lowest win rate (v3's looser rsi_oversold_threshold=52.19 fires ~3.6x more signals than DEFAULT_CONFIG's 45, most losing small, but its tighter stop/wider target (0.5/1.86 ATR) makes the wins large enough to keep sharpe_like from being worse). So v3 isn't ticker-overfit the way v9 was -- but all three configs are still net-negative on this unseen-ticker sample, a THIRD independent confirmation (after the entry-fill-timing and tune-vs-holdout findings) that this system's true edge, honestly modeled, is thin and not clearly positive out-of-sample.

=== STRATEGIC PIVOT (new top priority, supersedes pure parameter-tuning) ===

Context: after three independent rigor upgrades this session (entry-fill timing realism, tune-vs-ticker-holdout, v3-vs-ticker-holdout), the apparent edge kept collapsing toward zero from every direction rather than holding steady -- that pattern (each correction eating into the edge, all converging on the same "thin, near-zero-to-negative out-of-sample" conclusion) is the signature of a backtest edge that was substantially a measurement artifact, not genuine predictive signal being obscured by noise. Reinforcing evidence: v3's rsi_oversold_threshold has been pushed by every Optuna search (original tuning, candidate v5, v7, v9) UP toward 52ish -- barely below neutral (50), not a real "oversold" reading -- and v3 fires constantly (2021 trades on just 27 tickers in one test) at only a 20.88% win rate, staying near-breakeven only because of its asymmetric stop/target (0.5/1.86 ATR), a payoff shape that can look similar with zero real timing skill behind it. Conclusion, agreed with user: pure RSI-oversold mean-reversion is likely near its ceiling -- further parameter search within this same signal is unlikely to meaningfully improve things. Priority shifts to validating whether the CURRENT signal carries any real information at all, then (depending on that answer) adding genuinely different/complementary signal sources rather than continuing to retune the same one.

1. Random-entry benchmark (do this FIRST, before adding anything else). Reuse the existing harness (same stop/target/holding-period/universe/ticker-holdout discipline) but fire entries on random days instead of RSI-oversold days, and compare. If RSI-timed entries don't meaningfully beat random entries under the same rigor, that's near-proof the RSI signal itself carries no real predictive information and the returns we've been seeing come from market drift + the stop/target payoff asymmetry alone, not stock-picking -- which would mean layering more indicators on top of it is building on an empty foundation. Cheap (~20 min of new code, reuses everything already built), decisive, must come before deciding what to add.

2. If RSI shows some real edge over random -- ranked list of what to add next, cheapest/most-likely-to-help first:
   a. Volume confirmation on the signal day. Cheap (OHLCV volume already fetched, nothing currently uses it). Distinguishes "quiet drift lower" from "capitulation/panic-selling exhaustion" -- the classical mean-reversion setup that's actually likely to bounce, vs. a stock just quietly grinding down.
   b. Relative strength vs. sector/market. Buy dips only in names holding up BETTER than their sector/market during the pullback, not any oversold ticker regardless of context. Directly targets the "falling knife" failure mode already found this session (a stock making new lows for 19 days isn't a dip, it's a trend) -- RSI alone can't distinguish these, relative strength can.
   c. Momentum/trend-following as a second, independent strategy (not a tweak to mean-reversion -- buy relative-strength winners, not dippers). Equity momentum has more consistently documented out-of-sample edge in the literature than short-horizon RSI reversion. The harness (entry-fill realism, correlation-adjustment, ticker-holdout) is already strategy-agnostic and could validate this with identical rigor once built.
   d. External data (sentiment, options flow, analyst revisions). Highest potential lift, but a real architecture change (new ingestion pipeline, new data source) -- treat as a later phase, not next.

=== NOT DONE (older items, lower priority than the pivot above) ===

1. Automation (Phase 8). This is the practical daily-use pain point: miss a day of ingest.py/settle_trades.py and you have a silent gap in the training data, with nothing telling you it happened. Even before full Kubernetes, something as simple as a scheduled task plus a failure notification (email/Discord webhook on a nonzero exit code) would close most of this gap cheaply. Also directly useful now: more consistent daily scanning means future live samples span more/different market regimes instead of concentrating in whatever happened to be active when someone remembered to run it -- exactly the mechanism that made the 63-trade sample misleading.

2. Alerting. Right now you only find out a Strong Buy fired, a stop got hit, or a scheduled job failed by opening the dashboard or reading a terminal. A simple push (email/Discord/Telegram webhook) on ingest.py finding a new Strong Buy, settle_trades.py resolving a trade, or a Position Review flipping to SELL, would close the loop between "the system found something" and "a human notices," and pairs naturally with point 1's automation work.

3. Total portfolio exposure cap. The sector cap controls concentration within a sector, but nothing caps how much of your whole portfolio can be deployed at once across ALL sectors combined -- a natural sibling to max_sector_allocation_pct (e.g. max_total_deployed_pct, keeping some minimum cash reserve automatically rather than letting a very diversified day still deploy 100% of cash).

4. Risk-based position sizing. Shares_To_Buy is currently a flat dollar amount (Position Budget) divided by price -- the same $250 bet on a calm blue-chip and a violently volatile name. A more standard approach sizes each position so that (shares x ATR x stop_loss_atr_multiplier) is roughly constant across trades -- i.e. risk a fixed dollar amount per trade regardless of the ticker's volatility, not a fixed position size. Would make position sizing consistent with the risk-management thinking already baked into the stop-loss/RRR math.

5. Multi-objective tuning. Optuna currently optimizes a single scalar (sharpe_like). It's blind to tail risk -- a config could win by juicing consistency while quietly tolerating a nastier worst-case drawdown. Optuna supports multi-objective search natively; adding max-drawdown or max-consecutive-loss as a second objective (or a hard constraint) would make "winning" mean something closer to what you actually care about.

6. Re-run Optuna under the fully-corrected objective (entry-fill timing + correlation-adjustment + recency-weighting + ticker-holdout, all combined). v3's parameters were tuned before entry-fill timing realism or ticker-holdout validation existed, against a reward landscape that no longer reflects how trades actually fill or generalize. Three independent checks now agree v3's out-of-sample edge is thin-to-negative (entry-fill realism, tune-vs-holdout, and v3-vs-holdout above) -- deprioritized below the strategic pivot above: retuning the same signal further is lower-value than first confirming (via the random-entry benchmark) whether that signal carries real information at all.
